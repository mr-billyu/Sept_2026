Prayer + Sacrifice

Why This Matters

St. John Paul II, speaking about the terrible war then taking place in the Balkans, said that one of the most powerful forms of humanitarian intervention is prayer, especially when prayer is accompanied by sacrifice and suffering. He was speaking to people who could easily have felt helpless in the face of war, violence, and suffering. His message was essentially:

Christians are not powerless.

Even when we cannot personally fix a situation, our prayer, fasting, sacrifice, and suffering offered to God can participate in His work in ways that may remain hidden from us. Then John Paul II made the striking statement:

“Prayer joined to sacrifice constitutes the most powerful force in human history.”
St. John Paul II, General Audience, January 12, 1994

That is an extraordinary claim. And he was not alone in making it.

Saints and Popes Say the Same Thing

St. Thérèse of Lisieux

Decades before John Paul II, St. Thérèse wrote: “My strength lies in prayer and sacrifice; they are invincible weapons, and touch hearts more surely than words can do.” She called prayer and sacrifice “invincible weapons.” Her thought is remarkably close to John Paul II's.

St. John Paul II

In Evangelium Vitae he wrote that Christ showed us by His own example that: “Prayer and fasting are the first and most effective weapons against the forces of evil.”

Pope Benedict XVI

On Ash Wednesday in 2006, Pope Benedict XVI described the Christian struggle against evil as being fought with: “prayer, fasting and penance.” The Christian response to evil is not merely human effort. It includes spiritual weapons.

Pope Francis

In 2024 Pope Francis expressed almost exactly the same idea: “Prayer and fasting are the weapons of love that change history.” because they open our lives and the world to God's intervention.

There Is a Consistent Catholic Principle Here

All of these statements point toward the same idea. It is not simply: Prayer is powerful.

It is more specifically: Prayer + Sacrifice + Union with Christ = Powerful Intercession

The point is not that suffering itself has some magical power. The power comes from Christ. We unite our prayer, sacrifice, work, and suffering to Jesus and offer them to the Father through Him. That is why Catholics have traditionally practiced:

  • fasting
  • penance
  • offering things up
  • accepting sacrifices
  • offering suffering
  • making acts of self-denial
  • performing works of charity
  • offering daily duties

All of these can become part of our prayer.

So What Do We Actually Do?

The quotations above give us the motivation: Prayer joined to sacrifice matters. The rest of this sheet explains how to put that belief into practice in ordinary life.

1. The Basic Idea

Prayer and sacrifice belong together. Prayer says: “God, I ask You for this.” Sacrifice adds: “And because this matters to me, I offer You something of myself with this prayer.”

Then we unite both to Jesus: “Jesus, I join this prayer and this sacrifice to Your sacrifice on the Cross. Please use it according to Your will.” This turns an ordinary difficulty, inconvenience, act of self-denial, or suffering into a spiritual offering.

The Catechism teaches that our:

  • prayers
  • work
  • family life
  • hardships
  • ordinary activities

can become “spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.” (CCC 901; see also 1 Peter 2:5)

2. Why Does Sacrifice Add Something to Prayer?

Sacrifice gives prayer a cost. Suppose I pray: “Lord, please help my grandson grow closer to You.” That is good prayer. But I might also say: “Lord, today I will give up dessert and offer that little sacrifice for him.” Now my prayer involves not merely my words but myself. I am saying: “This intention matters enough to me that I am willing to give something of myself for it.” This reflects the way Jesus loved us. He did not merely pray for us. He gave Himself for us.

3. Jesus Is the Key

A Catholic sacrifice does not have spiritual power because suffering by itself is good. Its value comes from being united to Jesus Christ. Jesus' sacrifice on Calvary is the one perfect sacrifice. We do not add something missing from Jesus' Cross. Instead, Jesus allows us to participate in His offering. The Catechism says: Jesus desires to associate us with His redeeming sacrifice. See CCC 618.

St. John Paul II explains this extensively in Salvifici Doloris: our suffering can acquire a new meaning when it is consciously united with Christ's suffering. So think: My small sacrifice → united to Christ → offered to the Father → for someone or some intention.

4. A Very Simple Method

When you want to join sacrifice to prayer, use these five steps.

1. Choose the intention

For example:

  • conversion of someone you love
  • someone who is sick
  • a troubled marriage
  • peace in a family
  • someone struggling with faith
  • a deceased person
  • vocations
  • the Church
  • peace in the world
  • help resisting a particular sin

2. Choose the sacrifice

It does not need to be dramatic. It could be something you deliberately give up or something difficult that you did not choose.

3. Offer it to God

Simply say: “Jesus, I offer this to You for ______.”

4. Unite it to Jesus

Add: “I unite it to Your sacrifice on the Cross.”

5. What Can I Offer?

Almost anything legitimate can become an offering when done with love.

You might:

  • skip dessert
  • give up a snack
  • drink water instead of another beverage
  • fast
  • give up television for an evening
  • spend less time on your phone
  • give up an unnecessary purchase
  • get up promptly rather than lingering
  • take the less desirable portion of food
  • give money to someone in need
  • give someone your time
  • perform an unpleasant chore
  • do a task you have been avoiding
  • quietly let someone else have his preference

The sacrifice does not have to hurt very much. Small sacrifices offered with great love can be valuable.

6. You Can Also Offer Things You Didn't Choose

This may be even more useful in everyday life. Something unpleasant happens. Normally we think: “I hate this.” Instead, we can learn to think: “I didn't choose this, but since it is here anyway, I can give it to God.” Examples:

  • waiting in a long line
  • being stuck in traffic
  • aches and pains
  • poor sleep
  • bad weather disrupting your plans
  • an annoying task
  • disappointment
  • someone irritating you
  • being misunderstood
  • having to redo something
  • computer problems
  • loneliness
  • fatigue
  • an illness
  • limitations of old age
  • an unpleasant medical procedure

Rather than wasting the suffering, say: “Jesus, I offer this to You for ______.” The situation may not change. But what you do with it changes completely.

7. One of the Best Sacrifices: Doing Your Duty Well

Sacrifice does not always mean adding extra suffering. Sometimes the best sacrifice is simply doing what God has already placed before you. For example: “I don't feel like doing this, but it is my responsibility. Jesus, I will do it carefully and cheerfully for You, and I offer it for ______.” This can include:

  • household work
  • caring for someone
  • going to work
  • keeping an appointment
  • listening patiently
  • completing an unpleasant task
  • helping a spouse
  • keeping your temper
  • forgiving someone
  • praying when you don't feel like praying

Our daily duty itself can become an offering.

8. Sometimes the Greatest Sacrifice Is Interior

Not every sacrifice involves food or physical discomfort. Sometimes a much greater sacrifice is:

  • keeping quiet instead of having the last word
  • forgiving
  • not complaining
  • being patient
  • accepting correction
  • admitting you were wrong
  • giving someone your full attention
  • refraining from gossip
  • accepting another person's preference
  • treating an irritating person kindly
  • surrendering your own plan to God's will

These may cost us considerably more than giving up dessert.

9. Offer It Up

“Offer it up.” It does not mean: “Quit complaining and suffer.” It means: “Don't allow this suffering to be spiritually wasted. Give it to God and unite it to Christ for some good purpose.” Instead of merely: I am suffering. It becomes: I am offering. That is a tremendous difference.

10. Scripture Behind It

  • Romans 12:1 “Present your bodies as a living sacrifice.” Our lives themselves can become offerings to God.
  • Luke 9:23 Jesus tells us to: “take up his cross daily and follow me.” Notice: daily. Most of our crosses will therefore be ordinary ones.
  • Colossians 1:24 St. Paul says: “I rejoice in my sufferings for your sake.” Paul saw suffering united to Christ as something that could benefit others.
  • 1 Peter 2:5 Christians offer: “spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.” The phrase “through Jesus Christ” is crucial.

11. Prayer + Fasting

Jesus Himself practiced fasting. The Church has always connected: prayer + fasting + conversion. St. John Paul II wrote in Evangelium Vitae: “Prayer and fasting are the first and most effective weapons against the forces of evil.” Fasting reminds us that: I do not have to obey every desire I experience. And every time we experience the little discomfort of the fast, it can remind us of the person or intention for which we are praying. Hunger itself becomes a little prayer: “Lord, remember my intention.”

12. Sacrifice Does Not Bribe God

This is very important. We are not saying: “God, I suffered enough, so now You owe me what I asked for.” Sacrifice is not payment. Nor does it force God to grant a particular outcome. The proper attitude is: “Father, I ask for this because I believe it is good. I offer this sacrifice with my prayer. But I trust You to decide how this prayer should be answered.” Prayer joined to sacrifice is therefore also an act of trust and surrender.

13. Do Not Seek Harm

Catholic spirituality does not require us to invent dangerous suffering. Ordinary life provides plenty of opportunities. A sacrifice should never mean:

  • deliberately injuring yourself
  • neglecting necessary medical care
  • failing in responsibilities
  • harming your health
  • imposing your sacrifice on other people
  • becoming proud because you are sacrificing more than someone else

Remember: The purpose of sacrifice is love. Without love, the external act loses its meaning. The Catechism specifically warns that genuine sacrifice must come from the heart and be joined to love of neighbor. (CCC 2099–2100)

14. The Mass Is the Best Place to Offer It

One particularly Catholic practice is to bring these offerings mentally to Mass. When the gifts of bread and wine are brought forward, you can silently place your own offerings there too: Father, with this bread and wine I offer You my work this week, my difficulties, my prayers, my suffering, and the sacrifices I have made. Unite them to the sacrifice of Jesus.” Vatican II specifically teaches that the prayers, work, family life, difficulties, and sacrifices of lay people can be offered to the Father together with Christ's offering in the Eucharist. (See Lumen Gentium 34; CCC 901.) That makes the Offertory much more personal. You are not merely watching someone carry bread and wine forward. You are bringing your life to the altar.

15. A Simple Morning Offering

At the beginning of the day:

Jesus, I offer You my prayers, work, joys, difficulties, sacrifices, and sufferings today. I unite them to Your sacrifice on the Cross. Please use them for the people who need Your grace, especially ______. May everything I do today be done with You and for You. Amen.

Then ordinary life supplies the sacrifices.

16. A Five-Second Prayer During the Day

When something difficult happens, you don't need a long prayer. Simply say:

“Jesus, I offer this to You for ______.”

Or even:

“Jesus, for ______.”

God knows what you mean. This can gradually turn inconveniences throughout the day into prayer.

17. A Practical Example

Suppose someone you love has stopped practicing the faith. You already pray: “Lord, please draw him back to You.” Now add sacrifice.

  • Monday: “I'm skipping dessert for him.”
  • Tuesday: You encounter an irritating delay. “Jesus, I offer this frustration for him.”
  • Wednesday: You don't feel like praying the Rosary. “Jesus, I will pray it anyway and offer the effort for him.”
  • Thursday: Someone annoys you. Instead of retaliating: “Jesus, I will hold my tongue and offer this for him.”
  • Friday: You fast or abstain from something you enjoy. “Jesus, this is for him.”

Now prayer for that person has begun to permeate your whole life. That is prayer joined to sacrifice.

18. Why St. John Paul II Could Call It So Powerful

St. John Paul II said: “Prayer joined to sacrifice constitutes the most powerful force in human history.” He was not claiming that Christians possess some spiritual mechanism for controlling events. He was pointing toward something deeper. When we pray and sacrifice:

  • our weakness is joined to Christ's strength.
  • our suffering is joined to His Cross.
  • our love is joined to His perfect love.

We bring the person or situation to God while giving something of ourselves. The power does not ultimately come from our suffering. The power comes from Christ to whom we unite it.

19. The Whole Idea in One Sentence

Take whatever you must carry today, unite it to Jesus, and offer it with your prayer for someone who needs God's grace. Then very little in life needs to be wasted.

  • An inconvenience can become an offering.
  • A disappointment can become an offering.
  • A chore can become an offering.
  • A sacrifice can become an offering.
  • Suffering can become an offering.

And ordinary daily life can gradually become prayer.

Keep This Formula

PRAY — OFFER — UNITE — TRUST

PRAY
Tell God what you are asking for.

OFFER
Give Him some sacrifice, work, difficulty, or suffering.

UNITE
Join it consciously to Jesus and His Cross.

TRUST
Leave the result in the Father's hands.

“Jesus, I ask You for __.
I offer You ____.
I unite it to Your sacrifice on the Cross.
Use it according to Your will.
Jesus, I trust in You.”
