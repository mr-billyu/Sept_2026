Voluntas Ordinata

Ordering My Will to God’s Will

The Basic Idea

Voluntas ordinata means “ordered will.”

For the Christian, an ordered will is a will that is gradually brought into harmony with truth, goodness, love, and ultimately the will of God.

It does not mean that we stop having preferences, emotions, plans, hopes, or desires.

It means that those desires are put into the proper order:

“Lord, this is what I want—but more than anything, I want what is good, what is true, and what leads me closer to You.”

The goal is not merely to do God's will reluctantly.

The deeper goal is to become the kind of person who increasingly wants what God wants.

1. What an Ordered Will Looks Like

An ordered will says:

  • I will seek what is good rather than merely what is comfortable.
  • I will accept reality rather than constantly fighting what cannot be changed.
  • I will act where God has given me responsibility.
  • I will surrender what lies beyond my control.
  • I will trust God even when I do not understand what He is doing.
  • I will not confuse my preferences with God's will.
  • I will allow suffering, disappointment, and inconvenience to become occasions for love.
  • I will keep returning my heart to God when my desires become disordered.

An ordered will is not achieved once and for all.

It is something God forms in us over a lifetime.

2. God's Will and God's Permission Are Not the Same Thing

This distinction is extremely important.

Not everything that happens is something God positively wanted to happen.

God may will something.

God may permit something.

These are not identical.

God wills what is good.

But because He created human beings with genuine freedom, He sometimes permits things that are contrary to His will: sin, injustice, betrayal, cruelty, foolish choices, and their consequences.

God can also permit suffering arising from the fallen condition of the world.

Therefore:

“This happened” does not automatically mean “God wanted this to happen.”

But once something has happened, even something painful or unjust, God can work within that reality.

The Christian response becomes:

“Lord, I do not call this evil good. But since this is now part of my life, show me how to respond to it faithfully.”

Catechism

See especially:

  • CCC 302–314 — Divine Providence
  • CCC 311 — God is in no way the cause of moral evil
  • CCC 312 — God can bring good from the consequences of evil
  • CCC 1730–1742 — Human freedom
  • CCC 2822–2827 — “Thy will be done”

3. Four Things to Distinguish

When facing any problem, decision, disappointment, or suffering, it helps to separate four different things.

A. What God clearly wills

Some things do not require mysterious discernment.

God has already revealed that we should:

  • love God
  • love our neighbor
  • forgive
  • tell the truth
  • act justly
  • care for those entrusted to us
  • avoid sin
  • fulfill legitimate duties
  • pray
  • practice charity
  • seek holiness

We often spend time wondering about God's hidden will while neglecting His clearly revealed will.

A useful rule:

Do the clear good before worrying excessively about the hidden plan.

B. What God permits

Some circumstances enter our lives that we would never have chosen.

Examples:

  • illness
  • disappointment
  • another person's bad decision
  • rejection
  • aging
  • loss
  • plans that fail
  • unexpected responsibilities
  • inconvenience
  • suffering

We need not pretend these things are pleasant.

The question becomes:

“Now that this is here, how can I love God and neighbor within it?”

C. What is mine to change

Surrender does not mean passivity.

Sometimes God's will is that we do something.

We may need to:

  • solve a problem
  • seek medical treatment
  • apologize
  • repair something
  • confront wrongdoing
  • establish a boundary
  • ask for help
  • change a habit
  • make a decision
  • protect someone
  • work harder
  • rest
  • forgive
  • speak the truth

Before saying, “I surrender this to God,” ask:

“Has God already given me something to do about it?”

D. What is mine to surrender

Eventually we reach things we cannot control.

These often include:

  • the future
  • other people's reactions
  • whether others approve of us
  • whether our plans succeed
  • whether our efforts are recognized
  • many circumstances of health
  • the past
  • the consequences of another person's free choices
  • outcomes after we have done our reasonable best

At that point:

Faithfulness belongs to me.
Outcomes belong to God.

4. The Four-Step Practice

A simple way to practice voluntas ordinata is:

RECEIVE → DISCERN → ACT → SURRENDER

RECEIVE

Begin with reality.

Ask:

“What is actually happening?”

Not:

  • What do I wish were happening?
  • What should have happened?
  • Why isn't this different?

Simply recognize reality before God.

DISCERN

Ask:

“What is the good thing for me to do here?”

Consider:

  • What does love require?
  • What does justice require?
  • What are my duties?
  • Is there something I need to change?
  • Is there a sin I need to avoid?
  • Is someone depending upon me?
  • What would charity look like?

ACT

Do the good that is actually yours to do.

Do not wait until you understand God's entire plan.

Often we receive enough light only for the next faithful step.

SURRENDER

After doing what you reasonably can, give the outcome back to God.

Pray:

“Lord, I have done what seems mine to do.
I place what happens next in Your hands.”

Then return to your duty.

5. Preference Is Not the Same as God's Will

We naturally have preferences.

There is nothing wrong with that.

We can pray:

  • “Lord, I hope this works.”
  • “I want this person to recover.”
  • “I hope this opportunity opens.”
  • “I would rather avoid this difficulty.”

Jesus Himself prayed in Gethsemane while expressing what He desired.

The disorder begins when we silently add:

“And I cannot be at peace unless God gives me the outcome I want.”

An ordered desire instead says:

“Lord, this is what I desire. I ask You for it honestly. But I place it beneath my deeper desire to remain faithful to You.”

6. When You Trust God and Things Still Go Badly

Trusting God does not guarantee that events will turn out according to our wishes.

Trust means something deeper.

Suppose you:

  • prayed
  • tried to discern carefully
  • sought good advice
  • made a reasonable decision
  • acted honestly

and things still went badly.

Do not automatically conclude:

“I must have misunderstood God.”

You might have made the right decision and still encountered suffering.

Instead ask:

“Given what has now happened, what is the next faithful thing?”

The spiritual life often works this way.

God does not always give us a map of everything ahead.

He gives enough grace for the road immediately before us.

7. The Enemy of Voluntas Ordinata: “My Will Must Prevail”

Disordered will often appears in subtle forms.

“Things should not be this way.”

Perhaps they shouldn't be.

But if they presently are, constantly protesting reality can prevent us from responding well to it.

“I will be happy when…”

This postpones peace until circumstances cooperate.

“If God loves me, He will make this work out.”

God's love cannot be measured by how closely events follow our plans.

“I have to understand why.”

Sometimes understanding comes.

Sometimes it does not.

Faith may require obedience before explanation.

“I have to fix everything.”

Some burdens were never ours to carry.

“I prayed, so God should give me what I asked for.”

Prayer is relationship with God, not control over God.

8. Signs That My Will Is Becoming Better Ordered

You may notice gradual changes.

Instead of:

“Why is this happening to me?”

you begin asking:

“How should I respond?”

Instead of:

“I need everything to work out.”

you begin saying:

“I need to remain faithful.”

Instead of:

“I must control the outcome.”

you begin saying:

“I will do my part and trust God with the rest.”

Instead of:

“God must explain Himself.”

you begin saying:

“I may not understand, but I know whom I have trusted.”

Instead of constantly fighting reality, you become more able to say:

“This is where I am. Lord, meet me here.”

9. Voluntas Ordinata and Daily Duty

For most people, God's will is found far more often in ordinary duties than in extraordinary revelations.

God's will today may look like:

  • getting out of bed
  • saying your prayers
  • helping your spouse
  • calling someone who needs encouragement
  • preparing a meal
  • completing a task
  • taking care of your health
  • being patient with an irritating person
  • forgiving someone
  • doing necessary work
  • resting when rest is appropriate

Holiness is often hidden inside ordinary faithfulness.

A valuable question is:

“What has God placed immediately before me?”

Then do that well.

10. Voluntas Ordinata and “Offering It Up”

An ordered will also changes the meaning of suffering.

Instead of merely enduring suffering, we can unite it to Christ.

We might pray:

“Jesus, I did not choose this suffering, but since it is here, I unite it to Your Cross. Use it for Your purposes and help me carry it with love.”

Offering something up does not mean refusing legitimate relief.

You may:

  • take medicine
  • seek help
  • solve the problem
  • reduce unnecessary suffering

and still offer whatever remains to God.

11. Voluntas Ordinata and the Presence of God

Practicing God's presence helps keep the will ordered.

Throughout the day, briefly turn toward God:

“Lord, what does love require here?”

Or:

“Jesus, help me want what You want.”

Or simply:

“Thy will be done.”

These small acts gradually reshape the heart.

12. A Decision-Making Checklist

When unsure what to do, ask:

  1. Is anything clearly sinful?
    If so, reject it.

  2. What responsibilities has God already entrusted to me?

  3. What does charity require?

  4. What does prudence suggest?

  5. Have I sought reasonable advice when necessary?

  6. Am I being driven mainly by fear, pride, anger, comfort, or control?

  7. What choice allows me best to love God and neighbor?

  8. After deciding, can I place the outcome in God's hands?

Then make the best reasonable decision you can.

You do not need absolute certainty about every ordinary decision.

13. When Anxiety Appears

Anxiety often attempts to move our attention from today's responsibility to tomorrow's possibilities.

Ask:

What must I do now?

Do it.

What can I reasonably prepare for?

Prepare.

What cannot be solved now?

Give it to God.

Then return to the present moment.

A helpful prayer:

“Lord, give me grace for what is actually mine today.
Keep me from carrying tomorrow before it arrives.”

14. A Simple Examination of Conscience

At the end of the day ask:

RECEIVE

Where did I resist reality today?

DISCERN

Where did I recognize the good God was asking of me?

ACT

Where did I faithfully do what was mine to do?

SURRENDER

What am I still trying to control that I need to give back to God?

Then pray:

“Lord, forgive what was disordered in me today.
Strengthen what was good.
Put my desires in their proper order,
and teach me to want what leads me closer to You.”

15. Three Questions for Difficult Moments

When something suddenly goes wrong, remember only these:

1. What is the reality before me?

Accept the facts without immediately judging God's purposes.

2. What good is mine to do?

Do the next faithful thing.

3. What must I leave with God?

Release the outcome.

16. One-Sentence Rule of Life

Seek the good God gives me to do, accept the reality He permits me to face, and leave the results in His hands.

Or even shorter:

Receive. Discern. Act. Surrender.

18. A Short Morning Prayer

Lord, order my will today.

Help me desire what is good,
do faithfully what You place before me,
accept what I cannot change,
change what I ought to change,
love the people You place in my path,
and surrender every outcome to You.

Keep me from confusing my preferences with Your will.

When I do not understand Your providence,
help me trust Your goodness.

When I suffer, unite me more closely to Christ.

When I must choose, give me wisdom.

When I must act, give me courage.

When I must wait, give me patience.

When I must surrender, give me trust.

Jesus, teach me to want what You want.

Amen.

19. The Heart of Voluntas Ordinata

The goal is not to reach a point where we no longer care what happens.

The goal is to care deeply—but in the proper order.

We may desire health, success, security, friendship, family, comfort, and many other genuine goods.

But underneath them all grows a deeper desire:

“Lord, whatever happens, do not let anything separate me from You. Teach me to love You and to love well in whatever circumstances I am given.”

That is the beginning of an ordered will.

And eventually the prayer “Thy will be done” becomes less an act of reluctant surrender and more an expression of trust.

Not:

“I suppose I have no choice.”

But:

“Father, I choose to trust You.”

Quick Reference

When something happens:

RECEIVE — What is the reality?

DISCERN — What is the good?

ACT — What is mine to do?

SURRENDER — What belongs to God?

Remember

My responsibility is faithfulness.
God's responsibility is everything else.
